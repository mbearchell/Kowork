Welcome to Cowork

Stop prompting. Start delegating.

A working guide to Microsoft Copilot Cowork: what it is, when to reach for it, four demos you can run yourself, and the habits that separate a good result from a great one.

What is Copilot Cowork?

An agentic system that plans, executes, and delivers real work. You define the outcome; Cowork runs it end to end and returns a completed result, not just a draft or a recommendation.

Delegate complex work

Execute long-running, multi-tool tasks. Add guidance at any point and keep work moving while you are away, with checkpoints that keep you in control.

Built for real work

Grounded in your work context. Powered by the best model for the task. Connected to the apps and systems you use every day.

Work with confidence

Secure, compliant, and observable by design, applied to every task, every time. Built for cost control.

What makes it different

  • Delegate real work, not prompts. Cowork plans tasks, reasons across tools and files, and keeps work moving over time.
  • Grounded in Work IQ. It understands your work directly through Microsoft 365 rather than relying on connectors to stitch context together.
  • Connected to your tools. Plugins tap into the apps, data, and custom skills your team already runs on.
  • Observable and controllable. Transparent actions, reviewable progress, and guardrails to guide or stop execution.
  • Enterprise ready. Operates within Microsoft security, identity, and governance for confident adoption at scale.

Copilot Chat and Copilot Cowork: when to use each

Think of Copilot Chat as intelligence on tap: ask a question, get a draft, summarize something. Think of Cowork as Copilot taking action: you describe an outcome and it turns your request into a plan and works through it, with you at the controls.

Choosing between Copilot Chat and Copilot Cowork
 Copilot ChatCopilot Cowork
What it is Your secure AI for work — fast, intuitive, secure, and grounded in your data. Get answers, draft content, dig deeper and spark ideas. Your AI that gets the work done — a multi-task agentic solution that takes actions across your Microsoft 365 apps.
Best for Fast, focused tasks in one conversation: draft, summarize, brainstorm, Q&A, and singular deep research and artifact creation. Complex, multi-step work spanning multiple apps — end-to-end deliverables.
Speed Fast — response in seconds to minutes. Thorough — works autonomously through the full task for minutes to hours.
Task complexity Single-step or single-session. Multi-task workflows across tasks, applications, and sources.
Use when You need some help or assistance — a quick draft, a fast answer, an idea sparked, a quick email sent, or to dig a little more on a specific topic. You need Copilot to go do the work — multi-deliverable, end-to-end requests that need actions across apps, files and systems.
Scenarios Draft and iterate on content. On-demand daily briefing. Summary of a project status. Clean up your entire inbox and calendar. Multi-step workflows across systems. Cross-app workflows like preparing for meetings or business reviews.
The practical rule. Use Copilot Chat for as much as you can. When you hit its limits, or the task needs several deliverables and several apps, that is the moment to bump up to Cowork.

Demo 1 · I have been out for a week

The scenario

“I have been out for a week. I have untold numbers of unread emails. I need to get caught up and be productive in the next 30 minutes.” The same outcome is delivered twice: first by guiding Copilot task by task, then by handing Cowork the outcome and letting it work.

What this highlights

The difference between task-driven and outcome-driven work. Copilot needs three prompts and your judgment between each one. Cowork takes a single prompt and returns the briefing, the decisions, the deadlines, the drafts, and the plan together.

Microsoft 365 Copilot — three prompts

Prompt 1 · triage and group
Help me catch up on all unread emails in my inbox. Which messages require a response from me? Group these messages by topic or project.
Prompt 2 · draft the replies
Draft responses for the top five emails requiring my attention.
Prompt 3 · build the plan
Create a prioritized action plan for my first day back.

Copilot Cowork — one prompt

The whole outcome, delegated
I've been out of the office. Review my unread emails and identify everything requiring my attention. Create a catch-up briefing organized by priority, identify decisions I need to make, identify deadlines and commitments, draft responses for the highest-priority emails, and create a recommended action plan for today.
What to point out. Cowork plans before it acts, works across mail and calendar at once, and produces drafts you review rather than messages it sends. Nothing left the mailbox without you.

Demo 2 · Take my calendar back

The scenario

A full week of undifferentiated meetings. Cowork reads the whole week, classifies every meeting with real Outlook categories, protects focus time, adds buffer, and schedules a missing one-on-one. Then the plan changes mid-run.

What this highlights

Cowork writing back to Outlook rather than just describing your week. Approval cards before anything touches other people. And the moment that only works live: interrupting a running task and watching the plan rebuild around a new constraint.

Before you start

Create four Outlook categories with these exact names so the colors render: Customer, Internal Team, 1:1, and Optional.

Prompt 1 · delegate the calendar
Take ownership of my calendar for next week and get it into a shape I can actually work in.

Review every meeting and apply Outlook categories so I can see my week at a glance. Use only these four, exactly as named:
- Customer - customer meetings, demos, trainings, office hours
- Internal Team - team syncs and internal working sessions
- 1:1 - one-on-ones with my manager, peers, or direct reports
- Optional - meetings I could reasonably skip, delegate, or consolidate

Then fix the structure:
- Identify duplicate, overlapping, or redundant meetings and recommend which to consolidate or drop
- Protect at least two 2-hour focus blocks during my most productive hours, with [Focus] at the start of the subject
- Schedule a recurring 1:1 with my manager if one doesn't already exist, and category it as 1:1
- Add buffer where I'm back-to-back for more than three hours

Create a project plan first, then execute it. Apply the categories and make the calendar changes directly. For anything that affects other people, draft the message but don't send it. Tell me what you changed, what you categorized as Optional and why, and what you deliberately left alone.
The interruption · type this while it is still working
Hold on - stop. I just found out I'm traveling Wednesday and Thursday. Don't put any focus time on those days, move what you can off them, and I need the 1:1 with my manager before I leave, not after.
Backup interruption · a governance beat
Actually, don't decline anything on my behalf. Just category them as Optional and leave them on the calendar.
Prompt 2 · close the loop
Show me the before-and-after. How many meetings landed in each category, how many hours did you protect, what did you consolidate, and what's still waiting on me?
Prompt 3 · make it weekly
Set this up to run automatically every Friday at 3pm for the following week.

Each week: categorize the upcoming week's meetings using my Customer, Internal Team, 1:1, and Optional categories, protect two 2-hour focus blocks, add buffer where I'm back-to-back for more than three hours, and flag anything I should decline or consolidate. Apply the categories and make the calendar changes directly, but don't send anything to other people on my behalf - draft it and let me review.

Send me a short summary of what changed and what needs my attention.
What to point out. Interrupt early, while there is still work left to re-plan. Say “watch the plan change” out loud — the categories already applied stay, because they are still correct; only the scheduling steps get rethought.

Demo 3 · The supplier performance review

The scenario

A quarterly review of a struggling supplier. The scorecard, the standards document, the meeting notes, and the email thread do not agree with each other. Cowork investigates, reconciles the conflicts, and produces a decision-ready package for tomorrow's leadership review.

What this highlights

Autonomous project planning, data analysis with code, reconciling conflicting sources, four connected deliverables across Word, Excel, PowerPoint and email, and a self-review pass. This is the demo where Cowork does something Copilot cannot: it owns a whole business process.

Artifacts you need

A supplier scorecard workbook, a performance standards document, and quarterly review notes, plus an escalation email thread staged in the mailbox. Plant a few deliberate conflicts — a supplier claim the finalized data does not support, a projection that misses, and one genuinely missing value that must not be read as zero.

Prompt 1 · delegate the whole review
I'm preparing for tomorrow's quarterly performance review with Northstar Industrial Components. The relevant supplier scorecard, performance standards, meeting notes, and communications are available in Microsoft 365.

Take ownership of preparing the review. Create and execute a project plan that:
- Locates the relevant materials
- Analyzes the scorecard data, using Python if helpful
- Evaluates performance against our documented standards
- Identifies trends, anomalies, data-quality concerns, and differences between sites
- Reconciles the quantitative results with the issues and explanations contained in the emails and meeting notes
- Identifies the business risks, decisions required, and recommended corrective actions

Create a complete review package containing:
1. An executive decision brief in Word
2. A corrective-action tracker in Excel
3. A four-slide leadership-review presentation
4. A draft email to the supplier summarizing the issues and requested actions. Do not send it.

Verify that the metrics, conclusions, and recommendations are consistent across every deliverable. Clearly identify anything that cannot be verified rather than making assumptions. Continue working until the complete package is ready, and ask me only if you encounter a decision that prevents you from proceeding safely.
Prompt 2 · make it check its own work
Review the complete package as if you were the operations leader receiving it. Check the calculations, confirm that every conclusion is supported by the source materials, and ensure the recommended actions address the most significant risks. Correct any issues you find across all affected deliverables.
Prompt 3 · capture it as a skill
I love this output. Turn the process you followed into a reusable skill called Supplier Performance Review. It should work with other suppliers and scorecard formats, use the applicable performance standards, and produce the same review package. Ask only the essential questions needed to make the skill reliable and reusable.
Prompt 4 · prove it in a new session
Run my Supplier Performance Review skill for Northstar Industrial Components using the latest available scorecard and supporting materials. Prepare the complete package for tomorrow's leadership review. Do not send any communications.
Optional · one analysis, many tailored outputs
Now give me a one-page brief for each of the three sites, written for that site's plant manager, with only their numbers and their corrective actions.
What to point out. Name the discoveries as they appear: the supplier's claim that the finalized data does not support, the projection that came in short, and the blank field that has to stay blank. A network average can hide a site-level problem, and Cowork says so.

Demo 4 · Plan the offsite

The scenario

A one-page sponsor brief, a participant list, and last year's feedback become a complete offsite package: agenda, run-of-show, facilitator guides, kickoff deck, pre-work, tracker, calendar holds, and a planning chat. Then the venue takes a day away and every artifact re-derives together.

What this highlights

Everything at once — skills, scheduled prompts, approvals, clarifying questions, a mid-stream change, and artifacts across six surfaces. Cowork applies last year's lessons without being told to, because it read the feedback.

Artifacts you need

A sponsor brief that is deliberately silent on remote participation and confidentiality, a participant list with some attendees marked remote, and a prior-year feedback summary. Two venue messages are staged in the mailbox — a confirmation sent in advance and a change email you send live.

Prompt 1 · build the whole package
Dana Patel asked me to run the Q4 Operations Leadership Offsite, October 13-15 at the Lakeview Conference Center. Own the planning.

Start from three things in my OneDrive: the Q4 Offsite Sponsor Brief, the Q4 Offsite Participant List, and the 2025 Offsite Feedback Summary. Design this offsite so it fixes what participants complained about last year - don't repeat their mistakes. Use the venue confirmation email for rooms and capacity.

Create a project plan, then execute it:
1. A three-day agenda in Word: every session with its objective, format, owner, and duration, and a matrix showing which sessions serve which of the four objectives and who must be in each.
2. A run-of-show schedule in Excel: time blocks, room, facilitator, materials, AV, and buffers.
3. A facilitator guide in Word for every working session: purpose, prep, exercises, prompts, timing, and the output it must produce.
4. A kickoff deck: objectives, agenda, ground rules, and what success looks like on day three.
5. Pre-work assignments, drafted as emails - not sent - grouped by participant type.
6. A planning tracker in Excel with every task, owner, and due date counted back from October 13.
7. Calendar holds on my calendar for the three days and each planning milestone, plus a 30-minute planning kickoff next week with the planning team.
8. A Teams group chat called "Q4 Ops Offsite - Planning" with the same people; post the plan summary and ask for session owners.

Before you design sessions, ask me anything the brief doesn't settle - especially about remote participants and about what's confidential. Check with me before anything touches other people's calendars or Teams.
The interruption · send the venue change email first
Stop - the venue just emailed. The Main Hall is only available Tuesday and Wednesday; Thursday is gone. Compress this to two days without losing any of the four objectives. Merge or cut sessions, keep working time above 60%, keep the 75-minute cap, and keep the decisions session before lunch on day two. Then push the change through everything you've already built - schedule, facilitator guide, deck, pre-work, tracker, and my calendar holds.
Backup interruption · a scheduling constraint
Dana can only be in the room Tuesday morning. Move the FY27 priorities decision there and make sure nothing that needs her sign-off lands after noon.
Prompt 2 · capture it as a skill
That's exactly how I want every offsite and workshop planned. Turn this into a reusable skill called Offsite Planner. It should work for any team, any length, and any venue; always start from the sponsor brief and the most recent participant feedback; always ask about remote participation and confidentiality before designing sessions; keep working time above 60% and no session over 75 minutes unless the brief says otherwise; and never invite participants or send pre-work without my review. Ask only the essential questions to make it reliable.
Prompt 3 · the countdown
Every Monday at 8am until October 13, open the offsite tracker, check everything due in the next seven days and anything overdue, and send me a Teams message with what's at risk by owner. If a session still has no facilitator two weeks out, draft the ask to the best candidate from the participant list for me to review.
What to point out. The brief never mentions remote participation or confidentiality — so Cowork asks. And when the venue change lands, open the run-of-show and the facilitator guide side by side: same session, same new time, both documents. It did not rebuild; it re-derived.

Best practices

The habits that separate a good result from a great one. Most of them are about giving Cowork less to guess and more to work with.

Choosing your tool

Use Copilot for as much as you can

Most work is a question, a draft, or a summary, and Copilot Chat answers in seconds. When you hit its limits, or the task needs several deliverables across several apps, that is the moment to bump up to Cowork. Reaching for the heavier tool first is the most common way to make simple work slower.

Let Copilot help you write the Cowork prompt

Before a big delegation, ask Copilot to help you build the prompt: what to include, which model suits the task, and how much reasoning effort it needs. A few seconds spent shaping the request pays for itself across a run that lasts an hour.

Writing the request

Limit grounding as much as possible

Set time constraints, name the specific files, point at the folder. “Review my unread email from the last week” beats “review my email.” The narrower the ground, the faster and more accurate the result.

Describe the outcome, not the steps

Copilot wants a task. Cowork wants a destination. Say what “done” looks like — the deliverables, the audience, the decision it has to support — and let it work out the sequence.

Give it your rulebook

Point Cowork at the standard, the policy, or the template you already use, and tell it not to improvise where a rule exists. The difference between generic output and output your organization can file is almost always a document you already own.

Say what not to do

“Draft it, don't send it.” “Don't decline anything on my behalf.” “Ask me before you assume.” Negative instructions are as valuable as positive ones, and they travel with the work when you turn it into a skill.

Working alongside it

Interrupt — you do not have to wait

Plans change mid-run. Say so. Cowork keeps the work that is still valid, discards what your change invalidates, and rebuilds the rest. You do not need to stop it and start over.

Keep approvals on

Approval cards before anything sends, posts, or lands on someone else's calendar are not friction — they are the reason you can hand over real work. Resist “always allow” until you trust the pattern.

Ask it to check its own work

A second pass — “review this as the person receiving it, verify the numbers against the source, fix what you find” — catches more than a first pass ever does, and it corrects every affected file rather than just telling you.

Making it stick

Turn proven work into a skill

When a run produces something you would want again, capture it: “Turn this into a reusable skill.” The method, the business rules, and the guardrails are preserved, not just the output — and anyone who runs it inherits them.

Schedule what recurs

A skill is the how. A scheduled task is the when. Together they turn a process that depended on someone remembering into one that simply happens. Make the description self-contained: nobody is there to answer questions at 7am.

Start where the pain is

The best first candidate is a process you already dread: it starts with an email or a file, touches several apps, and ends with things in several places. That shape is where delegation pays off immediately.
